## 0. The story in 30 seconds

You hire a **trainee** to look after **₹1,00,000**. The trainee knows nothing about money.

1. You give them **history books** (old prices).
2. You build a **practice game** where each turn is one trading day.
3. The trainee plays the game again and again and **learns by trial and error** (reinforcement learning).
4. After every round, they take a **practice exam** on a year they never studied. You keep the version of them that scored best.
5. Then they take the **real exam** on two brand-new years, against a **lazy rival** who just holds gold every day.
6. A **report card** marks both.
7. A **waiter** (the API) serves the results to a **screen** (the dashboard) for everyone to see.

```
Yahoo Finance prices
      │
      ▼
```

## Chapter 1: getting the history (`havenrl/data.py`)

### 1.1 Which assets, and why these 3?

| Name | Ticker | What it's like |
|---|---|---|
| Gold ETF | `GOLDBEES.NS` | goes up when people are scared; can be bumpy |
| Liquid ETF | `LIQUIDBEES.NS` | almost cash; very calm |
| Bharat Bond ETF | `EBBETF0430.NS` | government-company bonds; slow and steady |

**Why these three?** They are all **"safe havens"**: places people put money to protect it, not to gamble. The project's question is "can an agent move money between safe places smartly?", hence the name **Haven**RL.

**Why ETFs?** An ETF is a fund you buy like a share. You can buy gold or bonds with one ticker, and Yahoo Finance has free daily prices for them.

**What does `.NS` mean?** It's listed on the National Stock Exchange of India.

### 1.2 Why does training start in 2020?

The Bharat Bond ETF **only started trading on 2019-12-30**. Before that there's no price, so 2020 is the first full year with all 3 assets.

### 1.3 `fetch_prices()`: download

- It downloads daily **closing prices** for all 3 tickers from 2020-01-01 to 2024-12-31.
- **`auto_adjust=True`:** prices are adjusted for things like dividends, so a jump isn't fake.
- **Why add 1 day to the end date?** Yahoo's `end` is **exclusive**: asking for "until 2024-12-31" stops at 2024-12-30. Adding a day includes the 31st.
- **`raw["Close"][TICKERS]`:** Yahoo returns a table with 2 header rows (price type, ticker). Taking only "Close" leaves one header row of ticker names. This fixed an old bug where the word "Ticker" ended up in the dates.
- **`dropna()`:** keeps only days where **all 3** have a price. Example: if Gold traded on a day but Bond didn't (data missing), that day is dropped, so the game never sees a half-empty day.

### 1.4 `daily_returns()`: prices → % moves

`pct_change()` turns prices into daily % changes.

**Example:** Gold ₹50 → ₹51 is a move of **+0.02** (+2%).

**Why moves and not prices?**
- A price of ₹52 alone says nothing. "+2% today" tells you what happened.
- Prices of different assets have totally different sizes (Gold about ₹50, Liquid about ₹1,000). Moves are all on the same scale (%).

**`fillna(0.0)`:** the very first day has no "yesterday", so its move is set to 0.

### 1.5 `split_periods()`: the three time periods

| Period | Dates | Trading days | Used for | School version |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Train | 2020-01-01 → 2021-12-31 | 498 | the agent learns here | homework |
| Validation | 2022 | 248 | choosing which saved brain to keep | practice exam |
| Test | 2023-01-01 → 2024-12-31 | 491 | the final score, used only once | real exam |

**Why split by time and not randomly?** Because in real life you only ever know the past. If you mixed days randomly, the agent could learn from a 2024 day and be tested on a 2023 day, which is learning from the future.

**Why do we need 3 periods and not 2?**
- Train only → you can't tell memorising from learning.
- Train + test → if you use the test to choose the brain, the test isn't "unseen" any more.
- Train + val + test → **val** is used for choices, and **test** stays clean for the final honest score.

### 1.6 Scaling: `fit_scaler()` and `scale_returns()`

**The problem:** the three assets move by very different amounts. Their usual daily move (std) in 2020–21:

| Asset | Usual daily move |
|---|---|
| Gold | 1.05% |
| Bond | 0.28% |
| Liquid | 0.04% |

To the neural network, Liquid's moves look like **zero**. A normal Liquid day (0.04%) is 25 times smaller than a normal Gold day.

**The fix:** divide each asset's moves by its own usual move. Now a **normal day is about 1** for every asset.

**Example:**
- Gold moves +1.05%, divided by 1.05%, gives **1.0** (a normal day)
- Liquid moves +0.04%, divided by 0.04%, gives **1.0** (also a normal day)
- Gold moves +3.15%, divided by 1.05%, gives **3.0** (a big day, 3× normal)

Now the brain can "see" all three equally.

**Why learn the usual move from train only?** If we used 2023–24 to calculate it, information from the real exam would leak into training (**data leakage**). It's like seeing the exam paper's difficulty before studying.

### 1.7 `prepare_data()`: everything in one call

Steps, in order:
1. Download prices.
2. Work out moves on **all** prices **before** splitting.
   - **Why before?** If you split first, the first day of 2022 would have no "yesterday" inside its period and would get a fake 0%. Working out moves first means 1 Jan 2022's move correctly uses 31 Dec 2021's price.
3. Learn the usual move from the train part only.
4. Scale all moves.
5. Return `{"train": (prices, scaled moves), "val": (...), "test": (...)}`.

**Why return both prices and scaled moves?**
- **Real prices:** used to actually buy and sell (rupees must be real).
- **Scaled moves:** used by the agent to *look* at the market.

## Chapter 2: the trading game (`havenrl/env.py`)

### 2.1 What is an "environment"?

In reinforcement learning there are always two sides:
- the **agent** (the player), and
- the **environment** (the game world).

They talk in a loop, every turn:
```
environment → agent :  "here's what you see"           (observation)
agent → environment :  "I pick basket 1"               (action)
environment → agent :  "here's your score, and what you see now"  (reward + next observation)
```

`HavenEnv` is built on **Gymnasium** (`gym.Env`), the standard library for RL games. **Why Gymnasium?** Every RL tool understands its two buttons, `reset()` (start a new game) and `step(action)` (play one turn). Using the standard means anyone can read it, and it could plug into other RL libraries.

### 2.2 The settings at the top, and why each value

| Setting | Value | Meaning | Why this value |
|---|---|---|---|
| `WINDOW` | 10 | the agent looks at the last 10 days | about two trading weeks: enough to spot a short trend, small enough to keep the input small |
| `START_CASH` | ₹1,00,000 | starting money | a round, realistic amount |
| `COST_RATE` | 0.001 (0.1%) | fee on every rupee traded | close to real Indian broker + exchange costs |
| `SWITCH_PENALTY` | 0.3 | score cut for changing basket | chosen by experiment (0.1 was too weak, see Chapter 7) |

### 2.3 The 3 baskets

| # | Name | Gold | Liquid | Bond |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | Mostly Gold | 60% | 20% | 20% |
| 1 | Mostly Bond | 20% | 20% | 60% |
| 2 | Mostly Liquid | 20% | 60% | 20% |

(The numbers are in `[Gold, Liquid, Bond]` order because that's the ticker order in `data.py`.)

**Why baskets instead of letting the agent pick any mix (like 47% Gold)?**
- **DQN can only choose from a short fixed list** of actions. It scores each option and picks the best. "Any mix" is an endless list, which needs a different, more complex algorithm.
- **3 choices are easy to learn** from only 498 days of homework.

**Why 3 baskets?** One "leaning toward" each asset. Each basket says "I think *this* asset is the place to be now."

**Why 60/20/20 and not 100/0/0?** Every basket keeps **at least 20% in each asset**, so it's never all-in on one thing (**diversification**). Even a wrong choice can't hurt too much.

### 2.4 The observation: why 34 numbers

The agent sees **34 numbers** each day:

**Part A: the last 10 days of moves = 30 numbers**
- 10 days × 3 assets = **30**
- These are the scaled moves from Chapter 1.
- **Example (shortened):** day −1: Gold 0.5, Liquid 1.1, Bond −0.3 … day −10: …

**Part B: where the money is now = 4 numbers**
- One number each for: **Cash, Gold, Liquid, Bond**
- Each is a share of the total money, and they add up to 1.

**Why 4 when there are only 3 assets?** Because money can also be **cash** (not invested). So there are 4 places money can sit.

**Example:** the agent holds Mostly Gold, worth ₹1,00,000 in total.

| Place | Money | Share |
|---|---|---|
| Cash | ₹0 | 0.0 |
| Gold | ₹60,000 | 0.6 |
| Liquid | ₹20,000 | 0.2 |
| Bond | ₹20,000 | 0.2 |

So Part B is **[0.0, 0.6, 0.2, 0.2]**.

**Why does the agent need Part B?** Because switching costs money. It must know what it already holds to decide whether moving is worth it. Example: already in Mostly Gold and picking Gold again costs almost nothing, but picking Bond means a switch, which costs the fee and the penalty.

**When is cash not 0?** Only at the very start of a game, before the first pick: **[1.0, 0, 0, 0]**, all cash. After the first pick, all money is always in a basket.

**Total: 30 + 4 = 34.** In the code: `WINDOW * n_assets + n_assets + 1` = 10×3 + 3 + 1 = 34.

### 2.5 `reset()`: start a new game

- `self.day = WINDOW - 1` = **day 9** (counting from 0).
  - **Why not day 0?** The agent needs 10 days of history to look at. Day 9 is the first day with a full 10-day window behind it (days 0–9).
  - **That's why the real exam starts on 2023-01-13, not 2023-01-02:** the first 9 trading days of 2023 are used only as "history to look at."
- Cash = ₹1,00,000, units of each ETF = 0.
- `self.basket = None`: no basket yet, so the **first pick is never a "switch"** and gets no penalty.

### 2.6 `step(action)`: one trading day, line by line

**Step 1: value before.** Count the money at today's prices.

**Step 2: rebalance to the chosen basket and pay the fee.**
```
traded = |target money in each asset − money in each asset now|, added up
cost   = traded × 0.1%
```
**Example: switching Mostly Gold → Mostly Bond with ₹1,00,000**

| Asset | Now | Target | Traded |
|---|---|---|---|
| Gold | ₹60,000 | ₹20,000 | ₹40,000 sold |
| Liquid | ₹20,000 | ₹20,000 | ₹0 |
| Bond | ₹20,000 | ₹60,000 | ₹40,000 bought |

Traded = ₹80,000, so the fee is ₹80,000 × 0.1% = **₹80** (0.08% of the money).

**Example: staying in Mostly Gold.** Prices moved a little yesterday, so the mix has drifted to maybe 60.3 / 19.9 / 19.8. Rebalancing back to 60/20/20 trades only about ₹600, so the fee is about **₹0.60**. Staying is almost free.

**Step 3: buy the units.** `units = basket share × (money − fee) / price`.
Example: ₹60,000 for Gold at ₹50 → 1,200 units.

**Step 4: next day.** Move to tomorrow and count the money again at tomorrow's prices.

**Step 5: the reward (score).**
```
reward = (value after / value before − 1) × 100
if the basket changed: reward −= 0.3
```
- **Why × 100?** It turns +0.005 into **+0.5**. Numbers around 1 are easier for neural networks to learn from than tiny ones.
- **Why is the fee already inside?** "Value after" is counted after the fee was paid, so the fee lowers the reward automatically.

**Example day:**
- Monday ₹1,00,000 in Mostly Gold. The agent switches to Mostly Bond.
- Tuesday ₹1,00,200 (+0.2%).
- Reward = +0.2 − 0.3 = **−0.1**. Lesson: "that gain wasn't worth switching for."
- If it had stayed in Gold and made +0.2%: reward = **+0.2**.

**Why the extra −0.3 penalty if the fee already costs money?** The real fee (0.08%) is small next to daily noise, so the agent didn't "feel" it. In the first run it switched **212 times**, and 212 × 0.08% ≈ **17% of the money lost to fees**. The penalty makes switching feel costly **while learning**, so it only switches when it's clearly worth it.

**Important:** the penalty changes only the **score**, never the **money**. The rupees only ever pay the real 0.1% fee.

**Step 6: game over?** `terminated = True` on the last day of data.

### 2.7 `info`: extra details

Each step also returns `info` with the **date**, the **money value** and the **weights**. The agent never uses it to decide. It's for us: charts, logs and results.json.

### 2.8 `play(env, choose)`: play a whole game without learning

It plays from start to end and returns `(dates, values, actions)`.
- `choose` is "who decides":
  - `lambda obs: agent.act(obs, greedy=True)` means the trained brain decides
  - `lambda obs: 0` means always basket 0 (the lazy rival)
- **Why one shared function?** Both the practice exam and the real exam use the same loop, so the agent and the rival are always measured the same way (a fair comparison).

## Chapter 3: the brain (`havenrl/agent.py`)

### 3.1 The core idea: Q-values

For each basket, the brain guesses a **Q-value**: "if I pick this now, how much total score will I collect today **and** in the future?"

**Example:** the brain sees today's 34 numbers and outputs:

| Basket | Q-value |
|---|---|
| Mostly Gold | 0.8 |
| Mostly Bond | **1.2** |
| Mostly Liquid | 0.3 |

It picks **Mostly Bond**, the highest. That's the whole decision.

**DQN = Deep Q-Network**: a neural network ("deep") that guesses Q-values.

### 3.2 `QNetwork`: the network's shape

```
34 inputs → 128 → 64 → 3 outputs
```
- **34 in:** the observation.
- **3 out:** one Q-value per basket.
- **128 and 64:** hidden layers that find patterns. Example: "Gold fell 3 days in a row and Bond is rising" → favour Bond.
- **ReLU:** a simple rule (negative numbers become 0) that lets the network learn curved patterns, not just straight lines.

**Why these sizes?**
- **Small on purpose:** only about 500 homework days. A huge network would just **memorise** them (overfit).
- **Funnel shape (128 → 64 → 3):** squeezes 34 numbers down to 3 decisions step by step.

### 3.3 `ReplayMemory`: the diary

Every day, the agent writes one line in its diary:
`(what I saw, what I picked, reward, what I saw next, game over?)`

- **Capacity 10,000 days.** When full, the oldest pages fall out (`deque(maxlen=...)`). One homework game is about 489 days, so the diary holds about the last **20 games**.
- **`sample(64)`:** it learns from **64 random pages**, not the last 64 days in order.

**Why random pages?** Days next to each other are very similar (yesterday is like today). Learning from them in order is like studying only Chapter 5 for a week: you forget Chapter 1. Random pages give a **mix**, so learning is balanced.

**Why 64?** A common batch size: big enough to average out noise, small enough to be fast.

### 3.4 The settings and why

| Setting | Value | Meaning | Why |
|---|---|---|---|
| `gamma` | 0.95 | how much future scores count | looks about 20 days ahead (1 / (1 − 0.95) = 20). Long enough to value "stay calm", short enough to stay focused |
| `lr` | 0.001 | learning rate: how big each correction is | the standard default for the Adam optimizer |
| `batch_size` | 64 | pages studied per lesson | see above |
| `epsilon_start` | 1.0 | 100% random at the start | try everything first |
| `epsilon_min` | 0.05 | always keep 5% random | never stop exploring completely |
| `epsilon_decay` | 0.95 | multiply ε by 0.95 after each game | slow, steady shift from exploring to trusting itself |

### 3.5 `act()`: pick a basket (explore vs exploit)

```
roll a dice (0 to 1)
if dice < epsilon → pick a random basket       (explore)
else              → pick the highest Q-value    (exploit)
```
**Why explore at all?** If it always picked its current favourite, it would never discover that another basket might be better. Like always eating at the same restaurant: you'll never find a better one.

**How ε changes over the 100 games:**

| Game | ε | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 0.95 | 95% random |
| 14 | ≈ 0.49 | about half random |
| 59+ | 0.05 | mostly trusts itself |

**Why decay per game, not per day?** The old notebook shrank ε **every day**. With about 500 days per game, exploring was over by about game 10, far too early. Per game spreads it over 59 games.

**`greedy=True`:** no dice at all, always the best guess. Used in **exams**, because you want its real skill, not random luck.

### 3.6 `learn()`: how the brain corrects itself

This is the heart of DQN. For each of the 64 random diary pages:

1. **The brain's guess:** the Q-value it currently gives the basket it actually picked.
2. **What the answer should have been (the target):**
   ```
   target = today's reward + gamma × (best Q-value tomorrow)
   ```
3. **The mistake (loss):** (guess − target)², averaged over the 64 pages (MSE).
4. **Correct the brain** a little to shrink the mistake (backpropagation + the Adam optimizer).

**Example:**
- It picked Bond. The brain guessed **1.2**.
- Today's reward was **0.5**. Tomorrow's best Q-value is **1.0**.
- Target = 0.5 + 0.95 × 1.0 = **1.45**.
- Mistake = (1.2 − 1.45)² = 0.0625. The brain nudges its Bond guess **up** for situations like this.

**Why "+ gamma × tomorrow"?** A choice is good if it pays **today and sets up good days later**. This is how the agent learns patience, not just "grab today's gain."

**`dones`:** on the last day there is no tomorrow, so the future part is multiplied by 0.

**Only after 64 diary pages exist:** `learn()` waits until there are at least 64 pages to sample from.

### 3.7 The target network: the steady teacher

There are **two copies** of the brain:
- **`q_net`:** the student, which learns every day.
- **`target_net`:** a **frozen copy**, used only to work out "best Q-value tomorrow" in the target.

**Why?** If the student made its own targets, then every time it learned, its targets would move too. It's like a dog chasing its own tail: it never settles. The frozen copy keeps targets still for a whole game, then gets refreshed (`update_target()` once per game in train.py).

### 3.8 `save()` / `load()`

- `save`: writes only the student brain (`q_net`) to `model.pth`.
- `load`: reads it back and also copies it into the target net.

The diary and ε are not saved, because exams don't need them.

## Chapter 4: the report card (`havenrl/metrics.py`)

Input: the money value on each day, e.g. `[100000, 110000, 99000, 120000]`.

### 4.1 `total_return`: how much it grew

```
(last / first − 1) × 100
```
Example: 1,20,000 / 1,00,000 − 1 = **20%**.

### 4.2 `max_drawdown`: the worst fall

For each day, remember the **highest value so far** (the peak). Today's fall is (peak − today) / peak. Max drawdown is the **biggest** of those falls.

**Example** with `[100000, 110000, 99000, 120000]`:

| Day | Value | Peak so far | Fall |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 1,00,000 | 1,00,000 | 0% |
| 2 | 1,10,000 | 1,10,000 | 0% |
| 3 | 99,000 | 1,10,000 | **10%** |
| 4 | 1,20,000 | 1,20,000 | 0% |

Max drawdown = **10%**.

**Why it matters:** it answers "how much pain would I have felt at the worst moment?" Most people quit a strategy after a big fall, even if it recovers later.

**The old notebook's bug:** it used (max − min) / max for the whole period. That counts a low that came **before** the high, and a low before a high isn't a loss.

### 4.3 `sharpe_ratio`: return per unit of bumpiness

```
daily moves = each day / day before − 1
Sharpe = average(daily moves) / std(daily moves) × √252
```
- **std** measures how much the daily moves jump around (bumpiness).
- **√252:** there are about 252 trading days in a year, so this turns a daily number into a **yearly** one (the standard way to quote Sharpe).
- **Risk-free rate = 0:** the textbook version subtracts what a "safe" deposit would pay. We skip it to keep things simple, and it's the same for both players, so the comparison is still fair.

**Example:** two strategies both make 20% in a year. A climbs smoothly, B swings wildly. A has the higher Sharpe, because it earned the same with less stress.

**Rough guide:** below 0.5 poor · about 1 good · 2 or more very good.

**If std = 0** (money never moved), Sharpe is set to 0 to avoid dividing by zero.

### 4.4 `summary()`

All of the above in one dict, rounded to 2 decimals:
`{'final_value': 116979.36, 'total_return_pct': 16.98, 'max_drawdown_pct': 3.42, 'sharpe_ratio': 1.48}`

**Why three metrics and not just return?** Return alone hides risk. A strategy can make 25% by riding one wild asset, or 17% safely. You need all three to judge.

## Chapter 5: training (`scripts/train.py`)

Run: `uv run python -m scripts.train`

### 5.1 Seeds: `SEED = 42`

Random things (dice rolls, starting weights, diary sampling) are made **repeatable** by fixing the seed in `random`, `numpy` and `torch`.

**Why?** Run it twice and you get the same brain. Without a seed, every run gives different results and you can't tell if a change actually helped. (42 is just a popular joke number. Any fixed number works.)

### 5.2 Setup

- `env`: the game on **2020–21** (homework).
- `val_env`: the game on **2022** (practice exam).
- First, the lazy rival plays the practice exam: always basket 0 on 2022 gives **₹1,08,955**. This is the bar the agent should beat on the practice exam.

### 5.3 The training loop

```
for each of 100 games (episodes):
    start a new homework game
    every day:
        pick a basket (dice < ε → random, else best guess)
        play the day, get the reward
        write it in the diary
        learn from 64 random diary pages
    after the game:
        ε = ε × 0.95             (explore a bit less)
        refresh the target net   (new steady teacher)
        sit the 2022 practice exam (greedy, no learning)
        if it's the best practice score so far → save the brain to model.pth
```

**Why learn every day?** More practice per game. Each day = one lesson of 64 pages.

### 5.4 Why keep the best practice-exam brain, not the last one?

Brains can get **worse** with more training: they start memorising homework (**overfitting**).

**Example:** game 30 scores ₹1.10 lakh on the practice exam, game 90 scores ₹1.02 lakh. Game 90 may be better at homework, but it's worse on new data. We keep **game 30**.

This is called **early stopping** (keeping the best checkpoint).

### 5.5 Why 100 games?

- ε reaches its floor (0.05) at game 59, so the last ~40 games are mostly the brain using what it learned.
- About 10 seconds per game on a normal CPU, so roughly 15–20 minutes in total.

### 5.6 What it prints

```
Episode  30/100  train Rs 1,32,000  practice exam Rs 1,10,500  epsilon 0.215  <- best so far, saved
```
(The numbers are illustrative.)

## Chapter 6: the real exam (`scripts/backtest.py`)

Run: `uv run python -m scripts.backtest` (after training)

### 6.1 What it does

1. Build the game on **2023–24**.
2. Load `model.pth` (the best practice-exam brain).
3. The agent plays once, **greedy** (no dice, no learning).
4. The lazy rival plays the same game: always basket 0.
5. Mark both with `metrics.summary()`.
6. Count the agent's switches.
7. Write everything to `artifacts/results.json` and print a short summary.

### 6.2 Why is the rival "always Mostly Gold"?

- It's the simplest thing anyone could do: buy once, never touch it (**buy and hold**).
- Gold is the strongest of the three in most years, so it's a **tough, honest** rival. Beating a weak rival (like "always Liquid") would prove nothing.
- If a complex AI can't beat "do nothing", it hasn't learned anything useful. That's the point of a baseline.

### 6.3 `count_switches()`

Counts the days the basket **changed**.

**Example:** baskets `[0, 0, 1, 1, 0]` → changes at 0→1 and 1→0 → **2 switches**.

**Why not count every day as a trade?** The old notebook reported "479 trades", which was really just the number of days. Switches are what cost real fees.

### 6.4 Why only once?

The real exam must stay **unseen**. If you look at the score, change something, and look again, you're slowly tuning to the exam (like studying the exact exam questions). So the test is used **once at the end**.

### 6.5 What's inside `results.json`

| Key | What | Example |
|---|---|---|
| `test_period` | the dates | `{"start": "2023-01-01", "end": "2024-12-31"}` |
| `baskets` | the 3 baskets | `[[0.6,0.2,0.2], ...]` |
| `agent` | the agent's report card + switches | `{"final_value": 116979.36, ..., "basket_switches": 102}` |
| `baseline` | the rival's report card + its basket | `{"final_value": 124769.96, ..., "basket": 0}` |
| `daily` | the money each day for both | `{"date": "2023-01-13", "agent_value": 100000.0, "baseline_value": 100000.0}` |
| `agent_actions` | the basket picked each day | `[0, 0, 1, ...]` |

**Why 482 daily values but 481 actions?**
- The test has 491 trading days. The first 9 are used only as history (Chapter 2.5), so the game runs on **482 days**.
- You **choose** on a day and **see the result** the next day, so 482 days have 481 choices in between.
- Like fence posts and gaps: 482 posts, 481 gaps.

## Chapter 7: the 3 experiments

| Run | What changed | Final ₹ | Return | Drawdown | Sharpe | Switches |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | first version: trained on 2020–22, kept the **last** brain, no penalty | 1,05,530 | 5.53% | 5.65% | 0.49 | 212 |
| 2 | + 2022 practice exam to pick the best brain, + switch penalty 0.1 | 1,10,927 | 10.93% | 4.71% | 0.94 | 153 |
| 3 ✅ | switch penalty 0.1 → 0.3 | **1,16,979** | **16.98%** | **3.42%** | **1.48** | **102** |

**Run 1, what went wrong:**
- **Overfitting:** it turned ₹1L into **₹2.5L on homework** but got only ₹1.05L on the real exam. It memorised the past.
- **Too much switching:** 212 switches × about 0.08% ≈ 17% of the money lost to fees.

**Run 2, the fixes:**
- The practice exam stopped the memorising.
- The 0.1 penalty cut switches to 153.
- Return almost doubled.

**Run 3:**
- A bigger penalty → 102 switches → fewer fees.
- The best return, the best Sharpe, and a drawdown **lower than the rival's**.

**Then we stopped.** Tuning more while looking at the test score would be cheating (Chapter 6.4). The final result was **locked** and reported honestly, even though the rival wins on money.

**The biggest lesson:** one number (the switch penalty) gave the biggest improvement. **Trading costs matter a lot** in trading RL.

## Chapter 8: the waiter (`api/main.py`)

Run: `uv run fastapi dev api/main.py`

### 8.1 What an API is

An API is the **agreed way to ask and answer**: a fixed menu. Ask `/summary` and you **always** get data shaped like `{"agent": ..., "baseline": ...}`. That fixed agreement (the **contract**) is the API.

- **My "supersonic" question:** the API is not defined by speed. A slow waiter is still a waiter. Speed is called **latency**, an important *quality* of an API, but not its definition. (More in NOTES_api.md.)
- The data travels as **JSON text**, not as a Python dict. Python turns the dict into text, and the browser turns the text back into data, so any language can read it.

### 8.2 The menu (endpoints)

| You ask | You get | Used by |
|---|---|---|
| `GET /` | the dashboard page | the browser |
| `GET /summary` | test period + both report cards (small) | quick numbers |
| `GET /results` | everything, including 482 days | charts |
| `GET /health` | `{"status": "ok"}` | "is the server alive?" checks |
| `/docs` | a free test page FastAPI makes automatically | you, for trying it out |

### 8.3 Choices and why

- **The waiter never cooks:** no training here, only reading `results.json`. Training takes about 17 minutes, and no user wants to wait that long for a page.
- **Read the file fresh on every request:** re-run the backtest and the new numbers show up **without restarting** the server.
- **Missing file → error 404 with a helpful message** ("Run: uv run python -m scripts.backtest") instead of crashing.
- **CORS:** a browser safety rule. A page from one address can't read data from another address unless the server allows it. We allow it (GET only), in case the dashboard is ever hosted somewhere else.
- **`RESULTS_PATH` built from the file's own location:** works even if you start the server from a different folder.
- **`/summary` is smaller than `/results`:** less data means a faster reply (low latency).

### 8.4 Words

- **Endpoint:** one menu item (`/summary`).
- **GET:** a request that only reads.
- **200** = OK, **404** = not found.
- **Decorator** (`@app.get("/summary")`): tells FastAPI "when someone asks this, run the function below."

## Chapter 9: the screen (`frontend/index.html`)

Open `http://127.0.0.1:8000/` after starting the API.

### 9.1 What's on it

1. **Period buttons:** All (2023–24) / 2023 / 2024.
2. **4 cards:** value at end, return, drawdown, Sharpe, each showing agent vs rival and who won ("▲ Agent better" / "▼ Baseline better").
3. **Line chart:** money each day for both. Hover to see both values for a day, and click a legend name to hide that line.
4. **Basket strip:** a coloured block for each run of days in one basket. Hover for the dates. The legend shows days per basket.

### 9.2 How it talks to the API

```js
const res = await fetch("/summary");  // ask the waiter
const data = await res.json();        // JSON text → data
```
- **`fetch`:** JavaScript's way to send a request.
- **`await`:** "wait for the answer" (it takes a moment, i.e. latency).
- If the API is down, the page shows "Could not load data from the API. Is it running?" instead of going blank.

### 9.3 Why plain HTML + JavaScript + Chart.js?

| Option | Why not / why |
|---|---|
| React / Vue | needs Node, npm and a build step: hundreds of files for one page |
| Streamlit | quick, but it skips the API, so the waiter would be pointless |
| **Plain HTML + Chart.js** ✅ | one file, nothing to install, one command runs everything, same address so no CORS trouble, clean frontend/backend split |

**The honest trade-off:** for a big app with many pages and logins, a framework becomes worth it. This is one read-only page.

### 9.4 The period buttons: how they work

When you click **2024**:
1. **Pick the days:** all 2024 days, **plus the last day of 2023** (2023-12-29) as the start. Like timing a runner's 2nd lap: you start the clock where lap 1 ended.
2. **Rebase:** both lines restart at ₹1,00,000 on that start day.
   - **Why?** The agent entered 2024 with ₹1,08,523 and the rival with ₹1,09,132. Starting them equal makes 2024 a fair race.
3. **Recalculate the 4 cards in the browser**, using the **same formulas** as `metrics.py` (tested: every number matched Python).
4. **Redraw** the chart and the strip for 2024 only.

**Why calculate in the browser instead of asking the API?**
- All 482 days were already loaded once, so the click is **instant**.
- A new endpoint (`/summary?year=2024`) would also be a good design, and it's how a future dynamic `/backtest` would work.
- **Rule:** the browser may do small maths on data it already has. The heavy work (training, running the agent) stays in Python.

### 9.5 Colours and access

- Agent = blue, rival = orange. Gold basket = gold, Bond = green, Liquid = purple.
- Colours were checked with a colour-blindness tool, and every colour also has a text label, so nobody depends on colour alone.
- Dark mode follows your computer's setting.
- Chart animation is off, so it draws instantly.

## Questions you might be asked

**Q: Why reinforcement learning and not just predicting prices?**
A: Predicting prices is hard and still leaves the question "so what do I do?". RL learns the **decision** directly, including the cost of acting (fees, switching).

**Q: Why DQN?**
A: The actions are a short fixed list (3 baskets), which is exactly what DQN handles. It's also the classic, well-understood RL algorithm.

**Q: How did you avoid cheating?**
A: Time-based splits, scaling learned from train only, model choice on 2022 only, the test used once, and tuning stopped after run 3.

**Q: Your agent lost. Why should I care?**
A: It reduced the worst fall by about 26%, beat the rival's Sharpe in 2023, and the loss is explained by a single gold rally. I also showed the honest result instead of tuning on the test until it "won."

**Q: What would you improve?**
A: More test years, several seeds averaged, more baskets or continuous weights (a different algorithm like PPO), and richer costs (slippage, taxes).

**Q: Why does the waiter never train?**
A: Training takes about 17 minutes. An API must answer in milliseconds, so heavy work happens offline and the API only serves results.
